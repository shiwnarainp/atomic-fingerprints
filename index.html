Atomic Fingerprints

How can scientists use the light emitted by atoms to identify elements?

Learning goals

Today I will investigate how excited electrons produce bright-line emission spectra and compare the spectra of different elements.

So I can explain how electron energy transitions produce specific wavelengths of light and how scientists identify elements by their spectral patterns.

I am successful when I can:
  • Explain how electrons absorb energy and move to higher energy levels.
  • Describe how electrons release energy as photons when returning to lower energy levels.
  • Explain why atoms produce specific colors of light.
  • Interpret a bright-line emission spectrum and compare spectra of different elements.
  • Identify an unknown element by comparing its lines with reference spectra.
  • Explain why each element has a unique bright-line spectrum.

Standards: NYS P-12 SLS HS-PS1-1 (patterns of electrons in atoms) and HS-PS4-4 (effects of electromagnetic radiation); HS-PS4-1 wave relationships as background. Color names follow the visible-light band on page 2 of the 2025 Reference Tables.

Module 5

A scientist discovers an unknown gas

A scientist points a spectroscope at a distant star. Starlight passes through the star's cooler outer gases, and those atoms absorb photons at exactly the wavelengths they would emit. The result is a rainbow with dark absorption lines. Their positions match the bright lines of each element's emission spectrum.

Where spectroscopy is used

  • Identifying elements in stars and nebulae from absorption and emission lines.
  • Checking gas discharge lamps: sodium streetlights glow yellow at 589 nm; neon signs glow red-orange.
  • Finding the composition of unknown samples, such as metals in water or soil (flame tests are a simple version).
  • Collecting spectral evidence in lab investigations, like the flame test lab in Unit 2.
